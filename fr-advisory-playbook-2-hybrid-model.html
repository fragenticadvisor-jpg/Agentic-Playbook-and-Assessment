Chapter 01
Why pure consumption
isn't the answer either
The mistake most SaaS businesses make when they decide to move away from seats is swinging to pure consumption. It solves the upside problem — but creates a new one: revenue volatility that destroys the ARR multiple that made you valuable in the first place.
Your investors, your board, and your finance team have built their models on Annual Recurring Revenue. ARR is predictable by definition — it recurs. The moment you shift to pure consumption, revenue becomes a function of how much your customers use the product each month. In a slow month, a slow quarter, a macro headwind — you find out how fragile that is.
Pure seat pricing caps your upside as agents displace human users. Pure consumption pricing destroys your floor. The hybrid model holds both. A platform base fee that protects your ARR floor and a consumption layer that captures upside as agentic usage grows.
"The hybrid model is not a compromise. It's the structural answer to a pricing problem that pure models — seat or consumption — cannot solve on their own."
FR Advisory
The three failure modes
Failure mode 1
Pure seats
Revenue decouples from value as agents displace users. NRR caps out. Expansion motion dies. Competitors who price on consumption eat your growth accounts.
Failure mode 2
Pure consumption
Revenue becomes volatile. Finance can't forecast. Investors reprice on a lower multiple. Enterprise buyers resist without budget certainty. ARR narrative collapses.
The answer
Hybrid model
Base fee = ARR floor + customer budget certainty. Consumption layer = upside capture + revenue that scales with value delivered.
What the market data shows
The SaaS businesses that have navigated this transition most successfully share a common architecture: a platform or access fee set at a level that covers their cost-to-serve and locks in a minimum ARR commitment, layered with a consumption tier that activates as agent usage crosses a defined threshold. The base is non-negotiable. The consumption is automatic.
The investor framing
When you present a hybrid model to a board or investor, lead with the base ARR floor — it protects the multiple. Then position consumption revenue as a new growth vector that didn't exist under the seat model. You're not replacing predictable revenue; you're adding a consumption layer on top of it. That's the story that gets funded.

Chapter 02
The four layers of a
well-designed hybrid model
A hybrid model isn't a single pricing decision — it's a stack of four design choices that need to work together. Getting one layer wrong undermines the others.
The Hybrid Revenue Stack
Base → Commit → Consume → Overage
Layer 4 — Overage
Usage above commitment ceiling
Priced at a premium rate above the included consumption allowance. Triggers automatic billing or prompts an upsell conversation. This is your highest-margin revenue — protect the rate.
↓
Layer 3 — Consumption allowance
Included usage within the commitment tier
Each tier includes a defined consumption allowance — tasks, runs, outcomes — priced into the tier fee. Customers know what they're getting and can plan against it. Drives natural tier-up at renewal.
↓
Layer 2 — Minimum commitment
Annual or quarterly ARR floor per tier
The commitment floor that makes revenue recurring. Customers commit to a minimum annual spend regardless of actual consumption. This is the ARR number you can take to the board.
↓
Layer 1 — Platform base
Access fee covering core product + infrastructure
A non-negotiable platform fee covering product access, infrastructure, support, and onboarding. This covers your cost-to-serve and anchors the pricing conversation away from pure consumption.
Layer design principles
LayerDesign principleCommon mistake
Platform base Set at or above cost-to-serve. Non-negotiable. Covers access regardless of usage. Setting it too low to win deals — creates a cost-to-serve problem at scale
Minimum commitment Expressed as an annual number. Tier-differentiated. Includes the consumption allowance. Making it negotiable in early deals — destroys pricing integrity for future accounts
Consumption allowance Generous enough that most customers don't overage. Creates value perception, not bill shock. Setting allowances too tight — customers feel nickelled and dimed, trust erodes
Overage rate Premium to per-unit tier cost. Triggers upgrade conversation at ~80% utilisation. Discounting overage to win deals — trains customers not to upgrade tiers
The commitment floor is non-negotiable
The most common execution failure in hybrid model rollouts is negotiating away the minimum commitment in the first few enterprise deals. It feels like a short-term win — you close faster, the customer feels like they won. But you've just trained your entire sales team that the floor is a starting position, not a principle. Every deal after that starts with a floor negotiation. Protect it.

Chapter 03
Tier structure design:
the three decisions
Most SaaS businesses default to three tiers because that's what they see everywhere. Three is often right — but the tier boundaries, allowances, and price points are decisions that need to be built from your usage data, not copied from a competitor.
Tier design has three core decisions: how many tiers, where to draw the boundaries, and how to price the gaps between them. Get the gaps wrong and you'll find customers stuck in the middle tier forever — no natural trigger to upgrade, no overage pressure, no expansion motion.
The three tier decisions
1
Tier count: almost always three
Two tiers force binary decisions — customers either fit or they don't. Four or more creates cognitive overload in the buying process. Three gives you a natural anchor (top), a natural default (middle), and a natural entry point (bottom). The exceptions: developer tools (more granular tiers work) and enterprise-only products (one tier, one price, custom).
2
Tier boundaries: set from usage distribution, not instinct
Pull your usage data and plot consumption distribution across your customer base. Your tier boundaries should sit at natural clustering points — not arbitrary round numbers. The goal: roughly 30% of customers in the bottom tier, 50% in the middle, 20% in the top. If your top tier is below 15%, it's priced too high. Above 30%, too low.
3
Price gaps: 2.5–3x between tiers, never less than 2x
The gap between tiers needs to be large enough to create a real decision moment. A 30% uplift between starter and growth doesn't justify the customer's internal approval process to upgrade. A 2.5–3x step creates a meaningful value proposition — and keeps your middle tier as the default anchor that most customers choose.

The three migration paths for legacy customers
Chapter 04
The legacy migration:
the hardest part of the transition
New logos are easy. You introduce the hybrid model, it's the only option, customers accept it or they don't. The hard problem is your existing base — customers who bought on a seat model and have no contractual obligation to move.
Migration failures follow a predictable pattern: a company launches a new pricing model for new business, leaves the legacy base untouched, and ends up with two parallel commercial architectures indefinitely. The new model never reaches critical mass. The sales team sells both. CS manages both. Finance reports both. The transition never actually happens.
Successful migrations are deliberate. They segment the base, define a migration timeline, and design a different path for each cohort — with clear commercial incentives to move before the deadline.

The migration communication framework
Every migration communication should lead with value, not mechanics. Don't open with "we're changing our pricing model." Open with "here's how your usage has grown and what that means for you." The commercial change is the second paragraph — and it comes with an incentive, not an ultimatum. Customers who feel rewarded for their loyalty migrate. Customers who feel ambushed churn.

The five objections you'll hear — and exactly how to handle them
Chapter 05
The five objections —
and how to answer them
Every consumption model transition surfaces the same five objections. They're not irrational — they represent real concerns from buyers who've been burned by unpredictable cloud billing. Here's how to address each one.
"I can't predict what I'll spend — I need a fixed budget."
+
This is the most common objection and the most legitimate. Enterprise buyers have budget cycles. Finance teams hate variance. The answer is in your model design — not your talk track. If your minimum commitment and consumption allowance are well-designed, 80%+ of customers should never overage. The bill is predictable because the model is designed for predictability.
Talk track
"Your minimum commitment covers [X] units per month — based on your current usage patterns, that's [Y]% more than you're consuming today. You can plan against a fixed number. If you grow beyond that, you'll see it coming in your dashboard weeks before it hits the invoice, and you'll have the option to upgrade your tier rather than absorb overage."
"I don't know what counts as a billable action."
+
Audit anxiety is a trust problem, not an education problem. Customers who ask this question don't need a longer explanation — they need a shorter, clearer definition. Your billable unit taxonomy should fit on one page, surfaced in-product, in onboarding, and in the contract. Binary definitions (did the agent complete the task? yes/no) beat nuanced ones every time.
Talk track
"A billable action is [one sentence definition]. Nothing else counts. You can see every billable event in your admin dashboard in real time, with a complete audit log. If you ever see something you don't recognise, there's a one-click dispute process — and we'll review it within 24 hours."
"We were on a better deal before — why are we paying more?"
+
This objection usually comes from legacy accounts being migrated, not new buyers. The mistake is treating it as a pricing negotiation — it's actually a value conversation. If the customer is running more agent workflows than they were when they signed their original deal, the value exchange has already shifted. Your job is to make that visible before you make the commercial ask.
Talk track
"Since you signed your original agreement, your team has automated [X workflows], which our data shows has saved approximately [Y hours/outcomes]. The new model is designed to reflect that value exchange — you pay more when we deliver more. And you have a grandfathered rate available if you move before [date] that locks you in at below our current list price."
"What happens if our usage drops — do we still pay the minimum?"
+
Yes — and that's by design. The minimum commitment is the ARR floor, not a consumption estimate. Frame it correctly: the commitment covers access to the platform and the infrastructure to run agents at scale. It's not a pay-per-use model for the platform itself — it's a subscription with a consumption upside layer. The minimum is what the subscription costs.
Talk track
"The minimum covers your platform access — the same way your SaaS licences today don't refund unused seats at year end. Think of it as the infrastructure commitment; the consumption layer is the upside when your usage grows. Based on your current run rate, you're tracking to [X]% above your minimum, so this isn't a theoretical concern for your business."
"Our finance team won't approve a variable cost line."
+
Finance teams approve variable costs all the time — cloud infrastructure, advertising spend, professional services. The issue is variance, not variability. Give them the tools to cap and forecast it: a hard spend cap in the admin console, monthly consumption alerts at 70% and 90% of allowance, and a historical usage report they can use to build the budget line in their model.
Talk track
"Your finance team can set a hard cap at any level in the admin console — we will never charge above it without explicit approval. Your cost line is bounded. We'll also send a monthly forecast based on your usage trajectory, so the number in their model is always current. Would it help to set up a 30-minute session with your finance lead and our commercial team to walk through the controls?"

Chapter 06
Your 60-day
hybrid model action plan
Designing a hybrid model is a 60-day process if done with urgency. The first 30 days are internal — data, design, alignment. The second 30 days are external — validation, testing, rollout planning.
W1–2
Revenue architecture audit
Model GRR and NRR risk under continued seat pricing. Build the scenario analysis that justifies the transition to leadership. This is your mandate document.
W3–4
Model design
Define the four layers: platform base, commitment tiers, consumption allowances, overage rates. Use the tier builder as a starting point. Design three tier options to test.
W5–6
Customer validation
Test the model with 5–8 accounts across adoption tiers. Validate tier fit, commitment levels, and objection patterns. Adjust allowances based on usage data.
W7–8
Migration planning
Segment the legacy base into three paths. Design the migration communication sequence. Brief CS and Sales. Set the rollout timeline and sunset dates.

What comes after this playbook
Revenue architecture sets the model. The next layer is operational infrastructure — the billing systems, telemetry pipelines, and CPQ structures that make consumption pricing operationally viable at scale. That's Playbook 03.
The window is shorter than it feels
The companies that transition their commercial model before the market forces them to do it retain pricing power. The ones that wait until seat growth has already collapsed are transitioning from a position of weakness — lower NRR, fewer expansion accounts, a legacy base that's already begun to erode. The hybrid model is not a future project. It's a current competitive decision.
