Chapter 01
The seat model is
structurally broken for agentic AI
Seat-based pricing was built for a world where software required a human to operate it. Agentic AI inverts that assumption entirely — and most SaaS pricing models haven't caught up.
For two decades, the logic held: more users meant more value. You charged per seat, per site, per entity — firmographic proxies that tracked organisational growth. When a customer hired more people, they bought more licences. The model was simple, predictable, and defensible.
Agentic AI breaks this logic at the foundation. Agents don't have seats. A single agent can do the work of ten users — or a hundred. Customers who deploy AI heavily can now extract dramatically more value from your product while their headcount stays flat or shrinks. Under a seat model, they pay the same. Or less.
"Every agentic feature you ship without a matching commercial model trains your customers to expect AI for free."
FR Advisory
This isn't a future risk. It's happening now in the cohort data of every SaaS business shipping AI features. Seat growth is flattening among your most agentic customers — not because they're churning, but because they're getting more value without adding headcount. Your GRR looks fine. Your NRR looks fine. But the structural decoupling is underway.
The core tension
Old world
Value tracked headcount
More users → more licences → more revenue. The pricing metric and the value delivery mechanism were the same thing: people using software.
Agentic world
Value tracks outcomes
Agents execute workflows, make decisions, and complete tasks autonomously. Value is delivered per action, per outcome, per workflow — not per seat.
The gap
Pricing hasn't moved
Most SaaS products now have agentic capabilities. Most SaaS pricing models are still priced on seats, sites, or entities built for 2012.
The opportunity
Capture value as it scales
A well-designed value metric means revenue grows with AI usage. Customers who extract more value, pay more. Automatically. Without a sales conversation.
What this playbook gives you
A clear framework for identifying the right value metric for your product — one that's transparent to customers, auditable by finance, and capable of capturing revenue as agentic AI usage scales. By the end, you'll know what to measure, how to validate it, and what to do next.

The three metric types — and what each one costs you
Chapter 02
The three types of
agentic value metric
Not all consumption metrics are equal. The right one for your product depends on what your agents actually do, what customers can observe, and what your infrastructure can meter.
Metric type What it measures Best for Risk
Action / Task
e.g. agent runs, API calls, tasks completed		 Volume of AI activity High-frequency, process-heavy workflows Customers can't control spend easily — triggers anxiety
Outcome / Result
e.g. deals closed, tickets resolved, docs generated		 Business results delivered Products with clear, measurable end-states Hard to define, attribute, and audit at scale
Compute / Resource
e.g. tokens, credits, processing time		 Infrastructure consumed Technical buyers, developer-facing products Opaque to business buyers — feels like a cloud bill
Deep dive: Action / Task metrics
The most common starting point. You charge per agent run, per workflow execution, per task completed. It's transparent, easy to meter, and customers can see exactly what they're paying for. The challenge is spend predictability — enterprise buyers hate open-ended consumption without caps or commit floors.
The fix: pair action-based pricing with a minimum commitment floor and self-serve spend caps. Customers get predictability; you get upside.
Deep dive: Outcome / Result metrics
The most defensible model when you can pull it off. You charge for the result — a resolved ticket, a qualified lead, a completed document. Revenue scales with the value delivered. The problem is attribution: customers will argue about what counts, edge cases multiply, and your audit trail needs to be bulletproof.
The fix: define a narrow, unambiguous outcome definition in the contract. Binary is better than nuanced — "ticket closed by agent with no human escalation" beats "ticket resolved satisfactorily."
Deep dive: Compute / Resource metrics
The natural fit for developer tools and API-first products. Technical buyers understand token costs. Business buyers don't — and won't. If your ICP is a CPO or CFO rather than an engineering team, resource-based pricing creates a translation problem that erodes perceived value.
The hybrid answer
Most mature agentic pricing models blend a base platform fee (predictability for the customer, ARR floor for you) with a consumption layer (upside capture as usage grows). The metric type you choose governs the consumption layer — not the entire model. Chapter 04 walks through the decision framework for picking the right one.

The signals that tell you your current metric is failing
Chapter 03
Five signals your
metric is decoupling from value
The seat model doesn't collapse overnight. It decouples slowly — first in your most agentic accounts, then across the base. These are the early warning signs.
📉
Seat growth flattening in your AI-heaviest accounts High urgency
Your top 20% of accounts by AI usage are no longer expanding headcount — but they're running more workflows than ever. They're getting more value and paying the same. This is the structural decoupling in its earliest form. Check it by filtering NRR by agentic adoption tier.
🔇
Customers not mentioning headcount when describing ROI Medium urgency
Listen to how customers talk about value in QBRs and renewal conversations. If they're citing tasks automated, time saved, or outcomes achieved — not users enabled — their mental model of value has already shifted away from seats.
💬
Sales team selling on ROI but billing on headcount Medium urgency
When your AEs pitch "10x productivity per employee" but invoice on seat count, you've created a value-price mismatch your customers will eventually act on. Expect pushback at renewal — or worse, a competitor who prices on the ROI story you're already telling.
🔄
Expansion conversations stalling despite rising usage High urgency
If customers are running 3x more agent workflows than last year but the expansion conversation goes nowhere — because there are no more seats to sell — your revenue architecture has structurally capped your NRR upside.
⚠️
Competitors launching consumption tiers Strategic signal
When your category's pricing language shifts from "per seat" to "per run" or "per outcome," buyer expectations shift with it. You don't need to be first — but you can't be last. A competitor's consumption launch is your 12-month clock.
How to check your own data right now
1
Segment customers by agentic adoption tier
Cut your customer base into three groups: high AI usage (top 20%), medium (middle 60%), low or none (bottom 20%). Use agent runs, workflow completions, or active agent count as the proxy.
2
Compare NRR across the three tiers
If NRR is flat or declining in your high-usage tier despite rising consumption, your metric is decoupling. This is your board-level data point.
3
Map the last 5 renewal conversations in each tier
What did customers cite as value? If the high-usage tier talked outcomes and the low-usage tier talked users, the language gap confirms the decoupling.
4
Document and present to leadership
Data beats opinion in pricing discussions. A three-slide summary showing the decoupling pattern by cohort is enough to move this from product team concern to board-level priority.

The four-question framework for choosing your metric
Chapter 04
The four-question
metric selection framework
There is no universally correct agentic value metric. The right one depends on your product, your buyers, and your operational infrastructure. These four questions narrow the field.
1
Can the customer directly observe it?
A good value metric must be visible and intuitive to the buyer. If your CPO or CFO can't open a dashboard and understand what they're being charged for, the metric fails the transparency test. Tokens and API calls fail for most business buyers. Tasks completed and outcomes delivered usually pass.
2
Can you meter it reliably and in real time?
Consumption pricing without robust telemetry is a commercial promise you can't keep. Before committing to a metric, confirm you can track it at the action or task level, feed it to billing without manual intervention, and surface it to customers in their own dashboard.
3
Does it scale with the value the customer receives?
The ideal metric grows when customers get more value and stays flat when they don't. Test this by asking: if a customer doubles their agent usage and doubles their business outcomes, does the metric double too? If yes, it's a strong candidate. If not, you've introduced a mismatch.
4
Can customers predict and control their spend?
Unpredictable spend is the primary objection to consumption pricing. Your metric needs to be forecastable — customers should be able to model their expected bill based on planned usage. Pair it with commitment floors and spend caps to address the budget anxiety that will come up in every enterprise deal.

Your 30-day action plan to validate and move forward
Chapter 05
Your 30-day
action plan
Choosing a value metric is a decision, not a project. This plan gives you a structured 30-day path from hypothesis to validated candidate — without derailing your roadmap.
W1
Internal audit
Map your agentic workflows, segment customers by adoption tier, and pull NRR by tier. Document the decoupling signal for leadership.
W2
Hypothesis definition
Apply the four-question framework. Define two or three candidate metrics. Score each against: observable, meterable, value-correlated, forecastable.
W3
Customer validation
Run structured conversations with 5 accounts — mix of high and medium agentic adoption. Test the metric hypotheses directly. Listen for confusion and resistance.
W4
Recommendation build
Synthesise findings into a one-page recommendation: candidate metric, validation evidence, infrastructure requirements, and proposed next step for leadership approval.
